Background

Mindful Grocery addresses the gap between brands and consumers amid growing interest in ethical products. Focused on supermarkets, it serves as a platform for brands to showcase their ethical supply chains and facilitates consumers' easy access to information, enabling choices aligned with personal ethics.

This group project followed the double-diamond design process, involving discovering and defining the challenge, developing ideas and solutions, prototyping and evaluating with sample users, and ultimately analysing feedback for future improvements.

Defining Problem

Background Research

My primary focus for this project was driven by the goal of promoting social equality and sustainability. After some initial research, I learned that capitalism has a negative impact on our world, particularly in terms of inequality in labour conditions and environmental emissions. Further research led me to believe that the absense of supply chain transparency could contribute to these issues.

Our initial research shows numerous organisations are dedicated to enhancing manufacturing and supply chain transparency for existing companies. Many large corporations also have internal systems for managing these processes. However, a significant issue seems to be the inadequate dissemination of this data to end consumers. Consequently, we have decided to focus our project on enhancing supply chain transparency to provide consumers with accurate and accessible data.

Concept Ideation

In this process, we employed the card technique to ideate the core concept of our design project. Cards were divided into 4 categories: data type, context, user population, and technology. Ultimately, we took turns blindly selecting a card from each category to generate possible scenarios. This approach allowed us to think outside the box and explore more creative possibilities for the design.

Employing this method along with some additional research, we pinpointed a specific challenge: the gap in connecting ethical information delivery with in-store grocery shopping. While various organisations advocate for ethical shopping, none seamlessly cater to in-store experiences. Recognising this gap, our team chose to develop a service that seamlessly blended into users' regular grocery routines. Thus, our design project centres on creating a channel delivering ethical data directly to shoppers, aiming to encourage more individuals to adopt ethical shopping practices.

Stakeholders Mapping

We foresaw potential stakeholders involved in our service by employing stakeholder mapping to understand their varying levels of engagement. While our ultimate goal is to influence labor rights and working conditions, we recognise that this impact will follow successful societal education. Therefore, in this context, our primary stakeholders will likely be grocery shoppers (our main target users), data analysts, and data visualisation designers responsible for delivering this informative data to end-consumers. These key players will play crucial roles in driving our service's initial adoption and effectiveness. changes

User Groups and Personas

We initiated the analysis of our potential users, grocery shoppers, by distributing a questionnaire on SurveyMonkey. After examining the responses, I developed a diagram illustrating various values and factors influencing individuals' shopping practices. This allowed us to identify patterns and categorise users into three groups: Beginners, Fairness seekers, and Global citizens. Design Objectives

Based on our user analysis, our design objectives are clear: provide detailed supply chain information for brands and products and educate users about labor rights and ethical issues. We aim to seamlessly integrate these objectives into the user interface, empowering shoppers with knowledge and encouraging more informed and ethical purchasing decisions.

Ideating & Developing Ideas

Sketches Ideation & Selection

With the card-sorting ideation, we ideated several potential design solutions with a primary focus on educating users about each product’s ethical data, whether during online or in-store shopping experiences. We tried to make sure that our service doesn’t suggest or exert pressure on shoppers to buy products from certain brands.

Following group ideation, I found that we can categorize these ideas based on different stages of the shopping journey. Each idea presents its pros and cons. We carefully compare each option, providing a rationale to determine whether to choose a particular choice.

I drew 2 potential scenarios, each with its own pros and cons. We decided on option A as it provides more flexibility in developing features that cater to user needs, aligning well with our design objectives.

With the storyboards, we were able to point out some key considerations that should be considered and developed further in the following steps:

  • Internet Connection
    The internet may weaken inside buildings, potentially causing delays in users promptly accessing information.
  • Mobile in Public
    Using a mobile phone in public can be risky, as users may not pay enough attention to their surroundings.
  • Prompt Responses
    During hurried shopping, it may not be convenient to read lengthy information about every item on the shelf.

These are some key considerations worth considering, in addition to the data bias concerns we’ve discussed as a group.

Delivery & Listening to Users

Low-fi prototyping & testing

I made a user flow based on the storyboard's selected options and used it to guide my team in building a low-fidelity prototype. The prototype specifically centred on the "during shopping" part of the journey.

Subsequently, we conducted usability testing sessions using the Heuristic Evaluation method to validate whether the design would have the intended impact on users' grocery shopping routines or if any issues needed attention and resolution. I organized the feedback using the card sorting technique on Miro, priotised each issue and planed for the next steps.

Ideal journey & storyboard

With the feedback, I presented a new user flow with features that provide more options in more scenarios when shopping. The features should include saving products to revisit saved items or articles on-site, especially for those who may not have time to go through them in-store. The "Mindful Grocery" app includes an AR scanner, News & Feed, Notifications, History, and My List, as outlined in the app's sitemap. This sitemap made in Miro ensures each feature matches what users want.

After refining a new ideal user journey, I created a new storyboard to ensure that everyone in the group shared the same vision of the design.

Visual design & hi-fi prototyping

Before creating a high-fidelity prototype with the new storyboard, I initiated the visual design of the app, including the logo and colour scheme for the interface. Using mind mapping, I developed a moodboard and sketched logos to establish the visual identity. Following that, I designed the app interface, aligning each feature with the previously created app sitemap.

The high-fidelity prototype was made accordingly with 5 main features:

  • AR Scanner: Upon entering the app, the 1st interface allows you to scan an item. You'll receive prompt information about it, and by swiping it up, you can read more or add it to your list for later review.
  • Feeds: Mindful Grocery compiles news about ethical shopping—blogs, trends, and company reports— being a one-stop source for users seeking information.
  • Notifications: The notification list constantly updates you on the items you have saved in your list.
  • History: You can always review the past items you've formerly scanned or searched, though you didn't save those items to your list. However, your activity won't be tracked unless you log in to your account. Additionally, you always have the option to turn off the tracker if privacy is a concern.
  • My List: Every item and article you save will be in [My List], which you can customise. These lists will be downloaded automatically as default settings, allowing you to view your list without Wi-Fi or Internet access. This option can permanently be deactivated.

Evaluating the Design

Design rationale

After done making the high-fidelity prototype, I went back to our initial design objectives to determine whether the final design adheres to the Design Objectives and addresses all user needs.

Result & Takeaways

With our limited time, this design still has many weaknesses that could be developed further. I critically reflected on my work in this project using the Responsible Research & Innovation (RRI) approach, encompassing anticipation, reflection, engagement, and considering possible future development.

An example of a critical issue that requires consideration is supporting individuals in the lower income bracket. Research indicates that certain small businesses may struggle to afford higher wages. Additionally, many immigrant labourers, facing limited employment options, may accept lower wages, posing a risk of both personal and business collapse if societal backlash occurs.

Ensuring the accuracy of information presented in the app also poses significant challenges. There's a risk of potential abuse to target specific brands, and this concern warrants careful consideration.

What can be done in the future

In order to sustainably support workers in the long run, policy changes and government assistance are required. Additionally, a more deliberate manner of collecting and providing data could potentially be applied, aligning with our purpose of improving working conditions not only for the labours of other entities, but also for our own employees.
