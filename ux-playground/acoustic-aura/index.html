Acoustic Aura

February – July 2024 · MSc HCI dissertation, Newcastle University

Enhancing theatre accessibility with vibrotactile stimuli

Acoustic Aura is a research-based project on the potential impact of vibrotactile stimuli on theatre experience and accessibility for D/deaf and/or hard-of-hearing spectators.

Abstract

Hearing impairment is a prevalent issue in the UK, particularly among older adults. Within the field of HCI research, various studies have investigated the use of technology, such as vibrotactile stimuli, to assist individuals with hearing conditions. However, the effectiveness of these accessibility solutions has not been thoroughly explored in the context of multimodal perception, where audiences typically receive information through multiple sensory channels in theatres and other public entertainment venues.

This study is a research-based project. It combines vibrotactile stimuli with other sensory inputs and evaluates its efficiency through prototype testing. Additionally, it explores the values and challenges faced by D/deaf and hard-of-hearing audiences in these settings to assess the potential of implementing vibrotactile stimulation. From this research, six key themes were identified, highlighting the essential aspects of the theatre experience and considerations for developing new technologies. These findings contribute to HCI knowledge and offer valuable insights for future studies aimed at enhancing accessibility for these communities.

Introduction

Motivation

Gradual hearing loss is a more common societal issue than many realise. It's a natural part of aging for many. My grandmother, for instance, has experienced a gradual decline in her hearing over many years. While people are generally understanding and helpful, the impact on her daily life, especially communication, is inevitable. This personal experience has sparked my interest in addressing this issue.

After coming to the UK, I attended a musical play at Newcastle University's Art School. There were two things I realised at the time: 1) most audiences were older adults, and 2) there was a BSL interpreter performing sign language at the side of the stage. It was my first time seeing a play that provided an interpretation, and it was absolutely intriguing. However, I soon realised — not only that I didn't understand the language — the constant switching of attention between the stage and the interpreter was quite overwhelming. This experience led me to question the accessibility of such performances for hearing-impaired audiences.

I conducted initial research on the D/deaf and hard of hearing (HoH) and the accessibility issues within the entertainment industry in the UK. It has been reported that 1 in 5 adults in the UK, or approximately 9 million, experience hearing loss. However, only about 150,000 individuals in the UK know British Sign Language (BSL), which represents only around 1.2% of the D/deaf and HoH communities.

Moreover, although there have been efforts to enhance accessibility and inclusivity for D/deaf and hard-of-hearing individuals in public entertainment venues, certain barriers may still hinder their engagement with performances. Despite technological advancements, Richardson (2018) noted that the number of D/deaf and hard-of-hearing theatregoers is lower than anticipated, suggesting that underlying accessibility issues still exist.

Research Intention

For this project, I was tempted to uncover the challenges of improving accessibility and inclusivity in theatres for the D/deaf and hard-of-hearing audiences. During the initial research, I came across numerous HCI studies that explored the concept of sensory substitution and cross-modality translation. Many of them demonstrate the prospect of leveraging tactile sensory to assist individuals with hearing conditions in music-related scenarios. Nonetheless, the studies were mostly assessed in lab-moderated environments where other potential stimulus or impediments in real-life scenarios was cut out.

In theatre scenarios, there are multiple stimuli an audience would experience simultaneously, such as on-stage acting, overlapping sounds and music, and even whispering from the crowds. While vibrotactile sensations show promise in enhancing experiences for D/deaf and hard-of-hearing audiences, each accessibility method has its strengths and weaknesses. Combining different features might improve the audience perception in theatres, but further research is needed to understand the full impact of integrating vibrotactile stimuli with other accessibility tools. Therefore, this project also aims to establish the significance of crossmodal composition when providing accessibility support and exploring the prospect of crossmodal translation, specifically auditory to tactile modal.

This project intends to contribute to qualitative empirical data, offering further insights into the experience of the D/deaf and hard-of-hearing theatregoers inside and outside the UK. It also produces a set of research artefacts comprising a vibrotactile transduced cushion pillow, vibro-audio file and video files. This contributes to the design consideration for future researchers and/or designers looking to enhance accessibility and inclusivity for the D/deaf and hard-of-hearing people, especially in theatre settings.

Approach & Methods

As a hearing individual, the researcher is well aware of a potential gap in understanding and fully empathising with the individuals of the D/deaf and hard-of-hearing communities. Therefore, this project favoured a participatory research approach to ensure the engagement of the target population, specifically people of the D/deaf and hard-of-hearing community, and to address their actual challenges and needs.

This project's procedure essentially followed the design thinking process, including empathising, exploring, and materialising. This framework helps me develop an accurate mindset when collating insights and considering plausibility in future designs for the D/deaf and HoH users, contributing knowledge to the HCI research discipline.

Research Methods

The research process is divided into 3 phases:

  • Empathising with the community (Interview and online survey)
  • Explore, Design & Prototyping
  • Evaluating the concept (Prototype evaluation session and post-interview)

Phase 1 - Empathising with the Community

This phase is about gaining an understanding of the standpoint of people from the D/deaf and hard-of-hearing communities. It primarily focuses on their experience when visiting theatres or attending other public entertainment venues, if any. I used the CSD matrix framework to help determine what was known and what needed further study. The matrix actively evolved with the researcher's growing knowledge and shifting questions throughout the study (see Figure 7). The emerging suppositories and doubts were addressed by engaging with the D/deaf and hard-of-hearing individuals through an online survey and in-depth interview sessions.

1.1 Online Survey

  • Published through multiple D/deaf and HoH communities on Facebook.
  • Acted as a screener for interested, voluntary research participants to conduct in-depth interviews, in which the data from the survey were used as the basis for further discussion with each participant subsequently.

1.2. In-depth Interview and Focus Group

  • A focus group session was conducted with a Deaf individual, the Head of a Deaf organisation and a BSL interpreter.
  • Other 4 interview sessions were conducted with 4 HoH individuals.
  • All of them experienced visiting theatres and other public entertainment venues while undergoing some hard-of-hearing conditions and voluntarily participated in this study.
  • They were informed about the study through an information sheet and signed the consent form before the interview sessions.

The data acquired in this phase essentially addresses potential barriers influencing the audiences' negative theatre experience when they have hearing conditions. Conversely, it also highlights the values upheld in theatre productions that contribute to their positive experience regardless of accessibility.

Phase 2 - Exploring, Designing and Prototyping

2.1 Ideations and idea selection

The data from Phase 1 revealed numerous insights that could be addressed to enhance the theatre experience of D/deaf and HoH audiences. The researcher used the sketch ideation method to explore plausible solutions and map the needs disclosed by the representative participants of this audience group (see Figure 8).

The insight led to the design concept that transduces acoustic signals to vibrotactile stimulation when music is incorporated. As a supplementary component, it offers the user another form of communication. To elaborate, the user would feel the vibration when the music starts playing, cueing them to the shift of events and/or stimulating sentiments that usually cannot be sensed through text features.

To evaluate this concept idea, the researcher created (i) a prototype that provides vibrotactile stimulations, (ii) a vibro-audio file that is tuned to the prototype and generates the vibration signal and (iii) four video cuts of pre-recorded play that would be incorporated with the vibrotactile stimuli during the concept evaluation phase (Phase 3).

2.2 Prototyping and encasing

The prototype was designed to examine the potential of using vibrotactile stimuli as a haptic cue to musical parts when seeing a play. Its electronic part consisted of an audio transducer, an amplifier, and a power supply, all positioned on a laser-cut wooden board (see Figure 9).

I specifically used a Dayton Audio TT25-8 Puck as the audio transducer for its ability to perform subtle vibration signals that shouldn't irritate the participant when testing. The audio signal was amplified and transmitted through a 5.0 Bluetooth mini amplifier connected to the audio source (e.g., smartphone) via Bluetooth. This setup was powered by a 12W power supply wired to the amplifier.

The prototype was developed as a cushion that could be temporarily attached to a chair for evaluation (see Figures 10 and 11). It consisted of layers of high-density foam, a laser-cut wooden board housing the electronic components, two sheets of inner textile, and an outer textile cover (see Figures 12).

2.3 Research Artefacts (a): Tactile-audio Files

The Dayton Audio TT25-8 Puck is a vibro-audio transducer that functions both as a speaker and a source of vibrations. However, for this prototype, it was important to generate only non-audible vibrations to avoid interfering with the lab-controlled hearing effects embedded in separate video files, which participants listened to through headphones. Since both sound and vibration frequencies are measured in Hertz (Hz), the frequency needs to be carefully manipulated to achieve the desired outcome.

The higher frequencies correspond to the faster vibrations and higher pitches of sound. However, the lower the frequencies, the more intense the vibration is perceptible on the skin. While human ears are capable of detecting audio spectrum ranging between approximately 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz, a significant speech spectrum impacting the listeners' intelligibility lines between about 500 to 8,000 Hz [source] (see Figure 13)

Therefore, by bypassing the speech spectrum, an essential amount of actors' speech and sounds was filtered out from the original audio file, leaving behind the spectrum representing acoustic instruments or musical scenes (ranging from 20 to 500 Hz). Based on the theory mentioned, the tactile audio files were prepared using Audacity audio editing software (see Figure 14)

These MP3 files would be directly transmitted to the prototype via Bluetooth, serving as the input source for the audio signals during the prototype evaluation.

2.4 Research Artefacts (b): Four video cuts of a recorded play

Unfortunately, while participants from Phase 1 were willing to contribute to this study through online interviews, physically engaging at Newcastle University's Open Lab was inconvenient. Hence, I asked some other HCI peers at the lab to take part in the prototype evaluation instead.

Given that, the audio of the video clips used in the evaluation had to be manipulated to simulate the desired hearing effects, i.e. deafening and hard-of-hearing conditions.

Simulating the 'hard-of-hearing' condition was more challenging, compared to the 'deaf' condition, due to its wide variety of types — each produces different auditory effects. I chose to replicate high-frequency hearing loss, which is reportedly more common with ageing and significantly impacts speech intelligibility [source].

This set of prototype and research artefacts are not intended to provide an immediate solution for theatre accessibility. Rather, they aim to evaluate the effectiveness and potential challenges of combining vibrotactile stimuli with existing supports, such as captions and hearing loops.

The goal is to identify any efficiencies, challenges, or risks that should be considered when developing and implementing these supports in the future.

Phase 3 - Concept Evaluation

The study predominantly explores the impact of cross-modal translation—specifically from auditory to tactile sense—on the theatre experience of audiences with hearing conditions.

Based on data from Phase 1, the focus evolved to assess the efficiency of vibrotactile stimulation when combined with other accessibility methods and its influence on audience perception.

It was unfortunate that I couldn't engage with the Deaf and HoH participants from Phase 1 in this experiment. However, four student fellows at Newcastle University were really generous and interested in helping me out with the study.

The evaluation consisted of four lab-moderated experimental sessions and mini-interviews following each session. They were conducted physically in the Open Lab at the Urban Science Building, where each participant watched 4 shorts from Disney's Tangled: The Musical while wearing headphones that simulated deafening and hard-of-hearing conditions.

The four experimental conditions included:

  • Experimental condition 1 - (Hearing = Deafening, Vibrations = no)
    This condition did not involve audio or vibration throughout the cut; only white noise was used in the background to emphasise the silence.
  • Experimental condition 2 - (Hearing = Deafening, Vibrations = yes)
    This condition only applied vibration but did not involve the play's audio throughout the cut.
  • Experimental condition 3 - (Hearing = Hard of hearing, Vibrations = no)
    This condition used the cut with edited audio of the high-frequency hearing loss effect; no vibrations were applied throughout the cut.
  • Experimental condition 4 - (Hearing = Hard of hearing, Vibrations = yes)
    This condition combined the edited audio with vibrotactile stimuli.

Each section lasted about 10 minutes and was followed by an interview discussing the impact of vibrotactile stimuli on four aspects of the viewing experience: (1) Comprehensibility, (2) Enjoyment, (3) Emotional Connection, and (4) Immersion.

The values of theatre performances as D/deaf and/or HoH audience

The data from both interview sessions in Phase 1 and Phase 3 were collated and analysed using the 'Thematic Analysis' method (using Nvivo).

Six key themes were identified, highlighting factors that hold a significant impact on theatre experiences of D/deaf and HoH audiences:

01Demand for deliberate technical implementation and proper maintenance.

This theme highlights the vital link between usability and accessibility. Participants emphasised the importance of deliberate technical implementation and proper maintenance to accommodate diverse hearing conditions, thereby enhancing the audience's experience. It encompasses topics such as adjusting acoustic and visual aids, addressing technical failures, ensuring technology is simple and intuitive, and effectively complementing the play.

There are 4 sub-themes mentioned and echoed throughout the interviews:

1.1. Deliberate adjustment of acoustic layers is vital to hard-of-hearing audiences.

1.2. Potential challenges are often caused by unforeseen technical errors.

1.3. The visual aid placement influences the perceptibility of the audiences.

1.4. Simplicity and intuitiveness are keys to improve accessibility.

02An inadequate accessibility to address the actual challenges.

This is another technical subject matter, underlining the issues with the inability to effectively address the difficulties experienced by D/deaf and HoH audiences through suitable assistance methods.

This can be divided into 2 primary issues:

2.1. Some sensory information is missing due to the gap in modal translation.

2.2. Audiences from diverse demographics have different requirements.

03The influence of other supplementary factors on the audience’s perceptibility.

The interview data predominantly indicates multiple factors that are relevant and/or influential to the perceptibility of the audiences aside of accessibility measures.

There are 4 sub-themes mentioned:

3.1. Individual’s habitual competence may aid their perceptibility differently.

3.2. Individuals may have different crossmodal perceptibility.

3.3. The demands for contextual cues to aid the comprehension of some audiences.

3.4. Accessibility features should be designed to passively support the audience's viewing experience.

04The influence of the on-set environments to the audience’s perceptibility.

This theme relates to the impact of human interactions and the overall live energy in public entertainment venues. It is essential because it passively influences accessibility in such environments.

4.1. The uniqueness of theatre lies in its live energy.

4.2. The accessibility barriers derived from the lack of public empathy.

4.3. More community involvement is required to effectively enhance accessibility.

05Pre- and post-experience: the audience's experience extends beyond the on-stage performance.

In addition to the physical theatre environments, the 'pre- and post-experience' also appears to be as crucial to the entire audience's theatre experience. This theme concerns the audience's journey before visiting a theatre, interacting with its staff members, and the conceivable post-service that could improve their theatre interactions altogether.

5.1. Raise awareness of the existing accessibility and ease the process of research for relevant information.

5.2. The audience-staff verbal conversation posts another challenge.

06Worthiness and affordability.

The value of investing in accessibility seems to vary among theatres and audiences with hearing conditions. All participants from Phase 1 mentioned the affordability of accessibility services and the worth of visiting a theatre at some point throughout the interviews.

The lack of accessibility in smaller or local theatres (as covered in theme 1) is often attributed to budget constraints and the perception that investing in such features may not yield significant returns, as only a small portion of the audience benefits. Participants highlighted that the high costs associated with specialised services—i.e. British Sign Language (BSL) training and relevant technologies—create barriers to accessibility. Concerns were raised about potential ticket price increases related to these investments, particularly given the already high cost of performances.

Despite that, all participants still agreed that implementing widely demanded features, such as captioning, should become standard practice to improve comprehension for hearing-impaired audiences. Additionally, prioritising simple accessibility measures, like accommodating seating arrangements, can enhance the theatre experience without requiring substantial resources, promoting greater inclusivity.

The Impact of Vibrotactile Stimuli on the Viewing Experience

The prospect of vibrotactile stimulation affecting the interaction of the audiences in the theatre was examined through 4 aspects of theatrical experience: comprehension, enjoyment, emotional connection, and immersion. The data from Phase 3 reveal a significant impact of vibrotactile stimuli on three perceptibility measures in both Deaf and hard-of-hearing conditions (see Figure 18). The vibrations also perform practically better when incorporated with auditory modal. Although the vibrotactile stimuli generally improved the peers' perceptibility, there were a couple of concerns echoed during the evaluation.

Comprehension

The study found that vibrotactile stimuli had a modest impact on comprehension, slightly increasing the median score from 6 to 7.5 in deafening conditions, with participants emphasising that captions were crucial for understanding when auditory senses were limited. One participant (PE3) compared vibrotactile stimulation to Braille, suggesting that it may be beneficial if further developed.

On the other hand, in hard-of-hearing conditions, the median comprehension score decreased from 8 to 7.5 with vibrotactile stimuli. One participant (PE4) highlighted its 'overstimulating' essence in an audible condition which became a distraction to the performance. Nonetheless, this distraction was mainly observed during the initial rounds of the prototype evaluation, leading to uncertainty about whether it was due to the learning curve of adapting to vibrotactile communication.

Enjoyment

While it helps improve the enjoyment in general, the vibrations appeared slightly more random without audio clues in the deafening experimental session. Two of them (PE1, PE2) experienced confusion due to its inconsistent pulses, which consequently, hindered their enjoyment in the first couple minutes to the performance. One participant (PE1) articulated that the vibration tempo did not match the act or "the performers' lips" on the stage as they expected.

Since the issues in both cases arose exclusively during the sad or emotional scenes, the absence of musical cues might have made the vibrations feel out of place, as they are typically associated with 'more exciting' emotions. This indicated a probable problem if the vibrations did not complement the visual cues or perform in an expectant way.

Regardless, there are still potential challenges with vibrotactile stimuli when acoustic features are present, as highlighted by another participant. During another participant's hard-of-hearing session, she reported decreased enjoyment when vibrations were present (PE4). She found the vibrotactile stimulation overstimulating, noting that its significant impact on comprehension led to annoyance, ultimately hindering her enjoyment.

Emotional Connection

The evaluation showed that vibrotactile stimuli significantly enhanced emotional engagement during performances, particularly under the 'deaf' conditions.

One participant (PE3) pointed out that the absence of tonal information weakened their emotional connections to the performance. However, vibrotactile stimuli seemed to fill this gap nicely, especially during scenes that aligned rhythmically with vibrations. Two participants highlighted that the play felt more enjoyable with vibrotactile stimuli emphasising the beat, particularly when the vibration tempo and the scene aligned well (PE1, PE2).

However, the results varied under the 'hard-of-hearing' conditions. The two native English-speaking participants (PE1, PE3) reported already having a strong emotional connection to the shows without vibrotactile stimuli, despite hearing challenges. In contrast, vibrotactile stimuli appeared to help the two non-native English-speaking participants (PE2, PE4) relax, focus, and enjoy the performance more. Although vibrotactile stimuli were 'overstimulating' and became an obstacle for PE4 in the previous 2 measures, the bass via vibrations appeared 'arousing' and 'helped add to what was missing' for emotional connection.

Immersion

Vibrotactile stimuli significantly impact immersion level, particularly concentration. The median score increases from 6 to 7.5 out of 10 under deafening conditions and from 7.5 to 9 under hard-of-hearing conditions.

Without the vibrations, two participants (PE3, PE4) revealed experiencing mind wandering during sessions under deaf conditions. Some participants (PE1, PE2)faced challenges engaging in the play under hard-of-hearing conditions due to poor audio quality and whisperings.

Although some participants (PE1, PE2) were slightly confused and distracted when first introduced to the new sense, the vibrations eventually helped them become more focused and immersed in the performance. Vibrotactile stimuli enhance their engagement, particularly well when synchronised with the performance.

Nevertheless, tactile stimuli can also be overwhelming and cause significant distraction, leading to a drop in immersion. Hence, considerate optimisation of such technology is crucial, just as other accessibility practices.

Discussion

This study presented the values and priorities of theatregoers with hearing conditions and examined the potential of vibrotactile stimulation to enhance their theatre experience. The findings suggest that a well-crafted environment significantly enhances audience engagement and enjoyment, creating memorable moments that solitary viewing cannot replicate. Since sound and music play a crucial role in eliciting emotional and physical responses [Siedenburg et al, 2024], audio-tactile translation was found to help compensate for the lack of musicality for D/deaf audiences, leading to greater enjoyment and engagement, consistent with prior research [Victoria, 2024, Kaufmann, 2015].

However, the present findings indicate the requirements of improving the theatre experience for D/deaf and hard-of-hearing audiences extends beyond vibrotactile stimuli. It shows a strong link between comprehension, enjoyment, and immersion, with full engagement often dependent on understanding the plot. While vibrotactile stimuli enhanced the overall experience, they barely aid comprehension and were most effective when combined with other accessibility measures like captions and hearing support. Hence, maintaining and improving comprehension-focused accessibility measures is essential.

The study also identified issues with existing accessibility options, such as improper system integration and overstimulation, which can detract from the experience. This highlights the need for deliberate, sensitive design and implementation, particularly when combining features. Given the varying perceptibility among individuals, offering customisable options and maintaining user control are crucial for ensuring inclusivity.

Because of restrictions in accessing the desired demographic, the investigation employed hearing HCI counterparts to scrutinize the vibrotactile notion, potentially leading to preconceptions. Upcoming studies ought to actively connect with D/deaf and hard-of-hearing societies to empirically evaluate multi-sensory awareness in diverse auditory situations. Delving into a broader array of cross-modal methods might further boost accessibility and inclusivity in theaters and other community entertainment locations.
