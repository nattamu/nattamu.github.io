Background

E-waste presents a growing environmental challenge marked by limited recycling and an escalating accumulation in landfills. PixelPlanet is a green app designed to address this issue by empowering UK citizens. It provides them with tools to actively participate in the UK government's established E-waste recycling system, hoping to make them an integral part of the solution.

This collaborative project involved two other design students from the MSc. HCI program at Newcastle University. Over three weeks, our research on e-waste issues guided us towards an iterative solution. Our goal was to consistently boost existing motivation to engage with the recycling system while ensuring amenities. This project isn't a case of a problem meeting a solution; rather, it represents an iterative process of actively engaging with the issue.

Phase 1: Discover

Understanding the problem

We initiated background research on e-waste issues based on the context of the UK and EU. Initial findings indicate that the UK government is trying to improve the repairability of electronic products through eco-design regulations and by providing numerous local recycling facilities for household e-waste to be recycled appropriately. Despite these efforts, hindrance factors, such as high repair costs and lack of information provided to local citizens, may discourage compliance, which poses a significant recycling obstacle and consequence as unclean items risk landfill disposal and exacerbate pollution.

State of the arts review

During the background research, we found many established green brands and systems that aim to improve the environment. I talked to several local people, drafted a user journey flow, and then plotted these found systems to determine the problem or gap in the flow that we could potentially improve in this project (as shown below).

Phase 2: Define

Determining the scope of focus

The journey map demonstrated the areas lacking at individual, organisational, administrative, and global levels, emphasising a lack of awareness and accessibility to e-waste recycling information and facilities.

This hinders people from engaging autonomously, as their motivation to contribute needs to be adequately supported. These difficulties could diminish people's motivation to participate in problem-solving.

Therefore, we developed a design concept to reinforce users' self-determined motivation to engage in recycling practice, raise awareness of sustainable consumption, and support knowledge-sharing within the community.

Phase 3: Develop

Ideating and selecting the design direction

In this stage, we began with a workshop using sustainability reflection cards to solidify our vision for this project. As shown in the picture, we picked five cards that represent our core values as a group, in which we agreed upon emphasising these 3 matters:

  • Assuring the longevity of the design
  • Promoting more sustainable behaviour
  • Utilising resources and existing materials to its best

Following the workshop, we ran a couple of brainstorming sessions, a quick sketch and a speculative design approach, each lasting about 10 minutes — to explore more design possibilities. Unfortunately, the ideas proposed during these sessions were somewhat vague, overly futuristic, or too tech-centric and did not effectively reflect the values we developed during our previous workshop.

We once again highlighted the potential of fostering people's intrinsic motivation to contribute positively to the community. We explored ways to create an emotional connection between individuals, the environment, and other non-human living beings. However, we also discussed the risk of using negative motivation—such as fear or guilt—to encourage social participation, which could ultimately undermine our design intentions.

After our first two unsuccessful attempts, I decided to refocus my efforts and revisit the key priorities from the workshop. To avoid creating unnecessary waste by utilising existing resources and materials, I found pursuing a simple mobile application could be a prudent approach. We also discussed some key criteria of this potential mobile application idea, such that it should be adequately user-friendly and engaging to reduce the likelihood of user abandonment.

This reminded me of a project I undertook during my undergraduate studies, which utilised the “edutainment” approach, blending education with entertainment. This method is particularly effective in scenarios where knowledge and information can be conveyed in a more enjoyable and engaging manner. I created a rough outline of the information that should be presented to users, along with the forms or methods of delivery. This process brought to mind nostalgic games like Pokémon and Tamagotchi.

Eventually, I proposed to the team a cute idea of developing an application with 3 approaches: (i) Gently reminding of fellow living beings, (ii) Informing helpful knowledge and data, and (iii) Providing necessary tools to support recycling practice — all delivered through a friendly digital pixel pet-pal.

Phase 4: Deliver

Determining user persona & storyboard

The idea was finalised to a community-based gamified recycling app that motivates individuals to participate in e-waste recycling. Users can foster a deeper connection with their environmental impact through their adorable digital ambassador, 'Paw Pal'. They would also connect with their friends, share progress, raise higher environmental credit for their neighbourhood, and collectively work towards a cleaner environment, fostering a sense of community.

To address the diverse needs of our potential users, we conducted thorough research and identified key user groups. These personas, representing different lifestyles, guided the development of our design features. We then created a storyboard that showcased two scenarios tailored to our target users' personas, incorporating the three dimensions you mentioned.

Wireframe & potential art style

The application could comprise 2 sections: (1) an archive of recycling places and instructions and (2) informative environmental reports.

Section 1: The archive of recycling places and instructions

Unlike plastic and cardboard, disposing of e-waste can be more complex and may not be accepted in standard recycling bins. Regarding the issue, the app provides a comprehensive archive of nearby recycling locations and detailed instructions for proper e-waste disposal. This includes guidance on disassembling items before disposal, if possible or needed.

Understanding user challenges, we've incorporated a delivery service option. This is particularly helpful for those living far from recycling centres or with large items like TVs. Users can track the delivery process directly within the app. Users will receive contribution credits once the e-waste is successfully sent into the UK official recycling system. This achievement is visually represented by a happy Pawpal and a more vibrant environment within the app.

Section 2: Informative Environmental Reports

The app features a pixel-style environmental scene inhabited by Pawpal. This scene dynamically changes based on the community's collective e-waste impact, reflecting either a landfill-filled environment or a thriving, recycled landscape.

Users also have personal credits reflecting their individual contributions to prevent discouragement among individuals in communities with lower scores. This personal ranking system encourages continued participation and motivates users to strive for a positive impact.

Beyond the visual representation, the app offers detailed recycling records in standard informative charts and reports. This transparent data provides users with a clear understanding of their community's progress and their own individual contributions.

Critical Reflection

The activities in the SHCI module helped us refine our core project concept and explore diverse design possibilities. Each seminar introduced us to compelling SHCI approaches, highlighting potential flaws in our ideas.

The features presented in the wireframes aimed to motivate users through a gamification approach, encouraging participation in creating a cleaner world by fostering a sense of competition. The system also offers a range of information and services designed to ease recycling practices. While this playful idea introduces greater transparency to social discipline and adds a fun element to recycling, it raises potential downsides or trade-offs that need consideration. For instance, offering delivery or pick-up services for e-waste may increase the carbon footprint, potentially negating environmental benefits.

Furthermore, the app may be used in various ways beyond the two personas introduced earlier. Although a competitive approach may inspire many to engage in recycling, it could also lead to frustration when visible progress is limited, thus contributing little to broader environmental goals. Additionally, some users might perceive extra information or reports as unnecessary or overwhelming. Unfortunately, time constraints prevented us from fully exploring these variations and limited our ability to empathise with a broader range of users. It would have been beneficial to examine other stakeholders in the system, expanding our design project beyond shaping recycling practices for the general public.

We recognised the importance of fostering interdisciplinary knowledge sharing to mitigate unintended consequences of our design. Thankfully, the Group Crit provided valuable feedback, identifying blind spots in our project and offering insightful suggestions for further exploration.
