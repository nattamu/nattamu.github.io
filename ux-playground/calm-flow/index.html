Enhancing wellness, enriching education

Overview

CalmFlow is a portable handheld device that aims to regulate the anxiety and stress of university students. It guides users through quick, efficient, scientifically grounded breathwork techniques. Through an intuitive design, it allows for immediate intervention, full focus and centremen with oneself during the experience. We propose the utility of this personal well-being device to collect biodata that can be used by University staff in an auxiliary manner to improve educational services.

This project intricately explored our foundational research, ideation phase and prototyping journey. We critically analysed the design's limitations and potential for future applications.

Foundational Research & Concept Ideation

“A smart city can be defined as a complex set of technology (infrastructures of hardware and software), people (creativity, diversity, and education), and institutions (governance and policy). Smart cities aim to create an environment that drives innovation...by fostering environmental and social wellbeing.”

Aktaş and İlgin, 2022

The domain of a ‘Smart City’ is evidently conducive to digital innovations focussed on improving wellbeing. Newcastle upon Tyne is home to these ‘state-of-the-art’ advancements, holding the status of UK’s smartest city in 2021. Where could we meaningfully place our intervention in such a rich hub of innovation?

For this project, we highlighted the gap in addressing general population health within smart city research, which typically focuses on healthcare and medical technology. Building on that, we explored the role of 'breathwork' in managing stress and anxiety, emphasizing the physical nature of these emotions. We connected our research to Soma Design, which prioritises a personal and sensory approach to design and focuses on students' mental health, particularly during exams. This study aimed to integrate themes of student wellbeing, breathwork, and data collection into a design concept while reflecting on the limitations and future potential of the design.

We primarily targeted university students as our main user groups to articulate our initial design notions as we could empathise more with the group, being students ourselves. We also speculated that the number of students using the exercise potentially illustrates the students' mental state, leading to the utility of this bio-data in improving educational services. Hence, we focused on the kind of shared technology that is sufficiently intuitive and effective for the user experience and data collection. Define Target Users & Finalise the Concept

To settle on the final design concept, it is essential to understand the nature and use of our targeted population, university students. I roughly determined two possible personas of students experiencing different types of stress — chronic stress and acute stress [Friedman, 2013] — that could affect the design requirements. Based on this, our design goals would be to 1) educate users on breathing techniques, 2) portability and intuitiveness, and 3) collect data that will help improve educational service.

Given the above reasons, we agreed that the handheld portable breathing companion was the best response to the requirements. The final concept had developed to an intuitive and effective separate device employing the notion of soma design to conquer the sense of connection, presence, and stillness [Hook, 2022], comprising two components: (1) the station and (2) the handheld gadget. Students can access these devices with their student cards, enabling the system to collect strictly necessary information, such as their stage of study and course name. The system would not see their name or ID, preserving the students' privacy, essential when dealing with sensitive domains such as mental health.

Develop & Prototyping

In this stage, I was responsible for the overall UX and developing the hardware components (encasing the inner components). I initiated the user flow to discuss with two programmers in our team (each responsible for a different area, highlighting in red and green), assuring everyone possessed the same picture of how the product should interact with the users and determining any concern that should be regarded and improved before moving on to the next stage.

Develop the 'Station'

The first component of the system was the 'station,' where multiple handheld devices were docked and ready for students to use. Students could scan their cards to check out a device when they needed to engage in breathwork exercises. It also served as the central hub where student data, such as program and year, along with device usage data, were collected once the handheld devices were returned to the station. The information would then be analysed and displayed on a web portal, which will be discussed later.

The flow started with the message 'Please scan your student ID' displayed on the screen, prompting the student to scan their card. Upon swiping the card, the system would verify the card's authority. If the student's data existed and was valid in the database, the screen would display, 'Please pick up a mediator,' and the LED beside an available mediator port would switch from green (available but locked) to amber, indicating it was ready to be discharged from the station. Once taken, the LED would turn red, indicating the port was empty. Otherwise, it would show an error, 'Student data cannot be found'.

After discussing with the programmer, we agreed to retain only the on-screen message and the card validation process, due to the project's limited timeframe. The LED lights would be mocked up solely to convey the concept during the final presentation. The final flow chart was developed to the one presented here.

Following that, I began to develop the encase of the station by creating iterative low-fi prototypes to determine the station's dimensions. In order to improve the ergonomics and visual appeal for users of different heights, one side was designed to slant slightly. After determining the final proportions, I created a drawing to finalize the sizing and ensure there were no conflicts with the internal components. Once confirmed, I prepared the laser-cutting file, cut the individual parts, and assembled them as shown in the final prototype below.

Develop the 'Handheld Device'

The other essential component was the 'handheld devices' that would provide a guide to breathing exercises for the student user, leveraging the 4-7-8 breathing technique. The project required us to use a Microbit for this particular part, posing some limitations that needed discussion with the programmer responsible for this part.

The final flow chart, as shown here, began with the student self-determinating when they found the need for the breathwork — pressing the left button to turn on the device. Then, when ready, pressing the right button, the breathing exercise would be instructed through a circular LED panel, showing 3 stages of a breathing set. As it required involving colours to sign the shift of breathing, the colour choice stemmed from the consideration of enhancing inclusivity to the colour-blinded users. Blue/orange, or alternatively blue-red, is the common combination believed to be the most colour-blind-friendly. Therefore, it led to the design of red indicating breathing in (4 secs); yellow indicating holding breath (7 secs); and blue indicating breathing out (8 secs). After the flow ended, the user could choose whether to restart the demonstration by clicking the right button. Otherwise, the device will shut off after 10 seconds (This was an add-on from the programmer to preserve the battery life for the presentation day).

The pulse rate was an optional feature on this device. There's been some association between a human's heart rate and mental state. With the meditator, the user could check their pulse rate before and after the breathing exercise by resting their finger on the pulse sensor at the side of the device. The data would be shown on the LCD screen whilst also being collated within the device, and it would be transmitted to the data hub once plugged back into the station.

After finalising the system flow with the programmer, I created the casing for the mediator following the same steps as for the station: starting with a low-fidelity paper prototype, generating the dimensional drawings, preparing the laser-cut file, and assembling the parts.

Deliver the Design

This project was introduced to fellow HCI students and professors at Open Lab, Newcastle University, where we presented our idea and collated more insights and critiques from other experienced designers to guide the plausible development of future work.

"CalmFlow aims to present students with a new breathing technique daily that can help them regulate their stress and anxiety on their own. Data regarding usage hours and times will be gathered via the portable device and presented on an admin web portal that can be viewed only by university staff members, who will use the data to improve student services in the future."

This concept was illustrated through the storyboard as shown below:

Aside from the two components (the station and meditator) introduced in the previous section, we also explored the potential of CalmFlow to serve as a useful auxiliary data source that could contribute to enhancing the educational system. Students' use of CalmFlow can reflect their mental states over the year. To communicate the idea, I was also responsible for designing the web portal — from planning the site map, determining the requirements of each site, and creating the website interface.

The data we presumed to be beneficial in monitoring the mental state of the general students and improving the educational services includes (1)the number of usage hours, (2) the specific date and time of usage, (3) the student’s year and (4) the student’s course or programme — wherein various types of charts and graphs were used to illustrated different data. I created a mock-up website in Visual Studio Code to demonstrate how a university staff in charge could use the portal to monitor the data — view in Github.

Critical Reflection

01Potential Challenges

Throughout the design journey, we had encountered several challenges in developing and/or implementing the design and system

1.1. Making sense of the bio data

A significant amount of research highlights the link between stress, anxiety, and pulse rate. We leveraged this connection to explore whether breathwork could help calm and alleviate stress. However, various factors beyond stress and anxiety can also cause an increase in heart rate, potentially affecting the accuracy of the biodata. This concern was raised by several HCI peers and professors during the demonstration at Open Lab.

To address this, we revised the user interaction design. Instead of having users wear the device 24/7, we developed a handheld version that allows users to choose when to engage in breathwork. The pulse sensor activates only when the user places their finger on it. While this approach may reduce the amount of data collected for the university to enhance educational services, it offers students greater privacy and minimizes the risk of inaccurate data.

1.2. Accessibility

Throughout the design process, we worked diligently to address potential challenges for student users with health conditions, particularly those with colour blindness. One limitation we faced was the Microbit's limited colour options for both its default screen display and circular LED light.

To accommodate this, we implemented multiple interaction methods, including circular lighting patterns and beeping sounds to indicate changes in breathing flow. Additionally, despite the restricted colour options, we carefully selected a blue-yellow colour scheme, which is considered more accessible for individuals with colour blindness.

1.3. Portability

Another challenge we encountered when using the Microbit and Raspberry Pi was the size and portability of the handheld device. Although the design was intended to be compact and easily portable—similar to a vape—the Microbit's size made this difficult. Additionally, at the time, it was not possible to wirelessly transmit data from the Microbit to the Raspberry Pi.

02Plausible Future Work

2.1. Context of Use

This design has widespread applicability. It could be beneficial to ideate how it might be used in other scenarios, such as with office workers. We believe this can penetrate areas of the ‘smart city’ beyond education. Ethnography would an extremely fruitful research method for this concept.

2.2. Data Ethics and Privacy

Given this design’s close connection to users’ mental health concerns, it’s essential to address and emphasize the security and privacy of data collection and storage.
